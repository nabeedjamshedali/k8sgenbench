# Literature Index — Reliability and Security Analysis of LLM-Generated Kubernetes Configurations

Compiled 2026-09-25. Every entry below was checked against a real source (arXiv abstract page, publisher/DOI record via Crossref, NCBI, or the downloaded PDF). Annotations come from the downloaded full text (abstract, method and results sections) unless marked otherwise.

**Themes:** (a) LLM generation of K8s/cloud configs and IaC benchmarks, (b) security of LLM-generated code in general, (c) K8s/IaC misconfigurations, security smells, defects and tool comparisons, (d) LLMs for K8s operations and security (detection, repair, hardening).
**Relevance:** 5 = directly shapes our design or is a baseline we must position against; 1 = background only.

**Venue status:** "arXiv" means a preprint with no confirmed peer-reviewed venue at the time of compilation. Several 2026 preprints are only weeks or days old, so cite them as preprints.

## 2. Annotations

### Theme (c) and (d): the four original papers

**[1] Rahman et al. 2023, TOSEM: Security Misconfigurations in OSS Kubernetes Manifests**
- *Problem:* Which security misconfigurations occur in real K8s manifests, how often, and in which objects?
- *Method:* Open coding plus a rule-based linter, SLI-KUBE (precision is 1.0 for most categories on an oracle set; hard-coded secret 0.82, insecure HTTP 0.93). 133 bug reports were submitted and 9 practitioners interviewed.
- *Dataset:* 2,039 manifests from 92 OSS repos (GitHub and GitLab, cloned Nov 2021).
- *Findings:* 11 categories: absent resource limit, absent securityContext, hostIPC, hostNetwork, hostPID, capability misuse, Docker-socket mounting, privilege escalation for child processes, hard-coded secret, insecure HTTP and privileged securityContext. 1,051 misconfigurations affect 13.9% of 4,707 objects. Larger manifests are more likely to be misconfigured (logistic regression). Of the 10 bug reports that got responses, 60% were agreed to be fixed.
- *Relation to us:* This is the reference taxonomy for human-written manifests and gives us a ready baseline and coding scheme for RQ3 (common misconfigurations). **Difference:** it studies human-written manifests only, uses static analysis only, and has no LLM or runtime component. We can map Kubescape/Checkov/Trivy rule IDs onto its 11 categories so our human-vs-LLM comparison is comparable with it.

**[2] Malul et al. 2024: GenKubeSec** (arXiv preprint. One search snippet claimed ACL 2024, but no DOI or proceedings record confirms it, so cite it as arXiv.)
- *Problem:* Rule-based K8s scanners have fixed rule sets and inconsistent labels. Earlier LLM approaches used generic API models.
- *Method:* They build a Unified Misconfiguration Index (UMI) of 169 misconfigs by merging Checkov, KubeLinter and Terrascan rules, with GPT-4 used for entity matching. Detection uses a fine-tuned CodeT5p-770M (GenKubeDetect). Localization, reasoning and remediation use Mistral with few-shot prompting (GenKubeResolve).
- *Dataset:* 276,520 K8s configuration files from The Stack's K8s subset, labelled by the three rule-based tools.
- *Findings:* Precision 0.990 ± 0.020 and recall 0.999 ± 0.026, matching or beating each rule-based tool. **Off-the-shelf pretrained LLMs (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Mistral) detected misconfigs poorly: best precision ≈ 0.81, recall ≈ 0.45, F1 ≈ 0.51.** An expert judged a sample of explanations 100% correct.
- *Relation to us:* It shows that generic LLMs have limited built-in K8s security knowledge, which supports the hypothesis that LLM-generated manifests will omit hardening. The UMI is a useful precedent for harmonising findings from several scanners. **Difference:** it covers detection and repair of existing files, not generation, and has no deployment test.

**[3] Ye, Le, Babar 2025, MSR: LLMSecConfig**
- *Problem:* Scanners detect K8s misconfigurations but do not fix them.
- *Method:* Checkov findings plus RAG context (Checkov policy source code and docs) are passed to an LLM repair loop. Each repair is re-validated by YAML parsing and a Checkov re-scan, with retries.
- *Dataset:* 1,000 configurations from the top ArtifactHub (Helm) projects.
- *Findings:* Mistral Large 2 achieved a 94.3% pass rate, 100% parse success and 0.024 introduced errors per file. GPT-4o-mini achieved only 40.2% pass and 99.8% parse, with 0.029 introduced errors and more repair steps.
- *Relation to us:* It shows large differences between LLMs on K8s tasks, which is relevant to our LLM-vs-LLM RQ, and it motivates checking for newly introduced misconfigurations. **Difference:** it is repair, not generation, uses a single scanner (Checkov), measures "functionality preserved" only statically, and never deploys.

**[4] Sgan Cohen et al. 2025: KubeGuard** (arXiv preprint. Note an unrelated paper also called "KubeGuard" at IEEE ICWS 2025; avoid confusing the two.)
- *Problem:* Static tools do not use runtime behaviour to enforce least privilege.
- *Method:* Prompt-chaining LLM workflows that take manifests plus aggregated audit, network (Hubble) and provenance (SPADE) logs. They create Roles and NetworkPolicies and refine Roles, NetworkPolicies and Deployments.
- *Dataset:* Two microservice apps (Google Online Boutique and AKS Store Demo) on a self-managed AWS cluster.
- *Findings:* GPT-4o scored F1 1.00 on Role creation, 0.953 on Role refinement, 0.961 on NetworkPolicy refinement and 0.929 on Deployment refinement. Llama-3.1-8B scored 0.808 on Role refinement and 0.793 on NetworkPolicy creation. Prompt chaining beat zero-shot and CoT, and log aggregation cut tokens by 99.96%.
- *Relation to us:* It is one of the few K8s-LLM works that uses a live cluster, and it uses realistic microservice demo apps that could be candidate projects for us. **Difference:** it hardens existing manifests from logs. It does not generate full deployments from a spec, compare against human manifests, or test failure recovery.

### Theme (a): LLM generation of K8s/cloud configs and IaC

**[5] Xu et al. 2024, MLSys: CloudEval-YAML**
- *Problem:* No benchmark existed for LLM generation of cloud-native YAML.
- *Method:* 1,011 hand-written problems (Kubernetes, Envoy, Istio), each with a reference YAML and a bash unit test that applies the YAML to a real cluster (Minikube-based workers, scaled to 64 machines). Metrics are BLEU, edit distance, exact match, key-value match and the unit-test score. Problems also have simplified and translated variants.
- *Findings:* Across 12 LLMs, GPT-4 had the best unit-test score at 0.515, GPT-3.5 scored 0.412 and Llama-2-70b-chat 0.085. Proprietary models led by a wide margin, code-specialised LLMs did poorly, and even GPT-4 made simple mistakes. Multi-sampling helped and few-shot prompting did not.
- *Relation to us:* It is the closest precedent for **functional, cluster-executed** evaluation of LLM K8s YAML, and the clearest one to cite for kind/minikube-based testing. **Differences:** tasks are short snippet-level problems, not whole real projects. There is no comparison with human manifests from real repos, no security or best-practice scanning, and no resilience or pod-kill testing. Its models (GPT-4 and Llama-2 era) are outdated.

**[6] Kon et al. 2024, NeurIPS D&B: IaC-Eval**
- *Problem:* No benchmark existed for LLM Terraform generation.
- *Method:* 458 human-curated AWS Terraform scenarios. Each has an NL prompt and a user-intent specification written in OPA Rego, evaluated with `terraform plan` plus the Rego checks.
- *Findings:* GPT-4 had the best pass@1 at 19.36%, compared with 86.6% on EvalPlus, which shows that IaC is much harder than general code.
- *Relation to us:* Its method of NL spec, generation and automated verification pipeline mirrors ours, and the Rego intent checks are an analogue to our spec-conformance checks. **Difference:** it covers Terraform and AWS, not K8s. It checks only correctness, not security or reliability, and makes no human comparison.

**[7] Lanciano et al. 2023, CLOSER: Analyzing Declarative Deployment Code with LLMs**
- *Problem:* Automate QA and best-practice recommendations for K8s manifests.
- *Method:* An ML pipeline that clusters manifests and fine-tunes or few-shot-prompts GPT-2-medium and GPT-J-6B to classify manifest quality. Labels came from Polaris (the `cpuLimitsMissing` check).
- *Dataset:* About 100 manifests from internal Nokia Bell Labs projects.
- *Findings:* This is a preliminary, feasibility-level study. It claims LLMs are "viable and promising" but reports no strong quantitative results, and the data was too small to use for clustering.
- *Relation to us:* It is an early precedent for using Polaris outputs as quality labels for K8s manifests, and it supports our choice of Polaris. **Difference:** it analyses human manifests with small pre-ChatGPT models and does no generation.

**[8] Pujar et al. 2023, DAC (invited): Ansible Wisdom (the precursor of Ansible Lightspeed)**
- *Problem:* LLM code generation for IT-automation YAML (Ansible).
- *Method:* A transformer (CodeGen-based) further trained on Ansible and YAML data. It introduces Ansible-aware metrics ("Schema Correct" and "Ansible Aware").
- *Findings:* The fine-tuned model reached BLEU 66.67 against 50.4 for few-shot Codex-Davinci-002.
- *Relation to us:* It is the canonical early work on LLM generation of YAML IaC. **Difference:** it covers Ansible, not K8s, measures only text similarity and schema, and does no security or runtime evaluation. This limitation motivates our execution-based pipeline.

**[9] Zhang et al. 2026, FSE: IaCGen / DPIaC-Eval**
- *Problem:* IaC benchmarks check syntax or intent, not whether the IaC actually deploys.
- *Method:* DPIaC-Eval has 153 real-world AWS CloudFormation scenarios (58 services). IaCGen is an iterative feedback framework (format, then syntax, then deployment errors) with actual deployment to AWS.
- *Findings:* Six state-of-the-art LLMs achieved only **20.8–30.2% deployment success on the first attempt**. IaCGen raised this to 54.6–91.6% within 10 iterations (Terraform generalisation: 79.7% passItr@1 and 100% passItr@7). **Trustworthiness remained low: 25.2% user-requirement coverage and an 8.4% security-compliance rate.**
- *Relation to us:* This is the strongest evidence that syntactic validity does not imply deployability, and that deployable LLM IaC is still largely insecure. It directly motivates our runtime-deployment stage. **Difference:** it covers CloudFormation and AWS, not K8s. It has no human baseline from the same projects and no resilience or recovery testing.

**[10] Jana et al. 2026, ICSE-SEIP: TerraFormer**
- *Problem:* LLMs hallucinate Terraform resources and attributes.
- *Method:* Supervised fine-tuning plus RL guided by formal verifiers (`terraform validate`, `terraform plan`, OPA/Rego policy checks). It builds the TF-Gen (152K) and TF-Mutn (52K) datasets.
- *Findings:* Gains over the base model of +15.94% on IaC-Eval, +11.65% on TF-Gen and +19.60% on TF-Mutn, compared against 17 state-of-the-art models.
- *Relation to us:* It shows the field treating a multi-stage verifier chain (syntax, plan or deploy, policy) as the right way to judge LLM IaC, which supports our layered pipeline. **Difference:** it covers Terraform, is a technique rather than an empirical study, and has no K8s or human-vs-LLM comparison.

**[11] Santos Vargas et al. 2026, SBSeg (accepted): Security-First Evaluation of Text-to-Terraform**
- *Problem:* Can LLMs and SLMs produce *secure* Terraform, not just valid Terraform?
- *Method:* 7 models: Claude Opus 4, GPT-5.4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen2.5-Coder-14B, WizardCoder-33B, CodeLlama-13B and Magicoder-7B. 17 AWS scenarios (S3, KMS, VPC) drawn from IaC-Eval. Output goes through `terraform validate`, Checkov and Trivy in a GitLab CI pipeline. Two prompt strategies are tested at three security levels (pass@5), plus scanner-feedback self-correction.
- *Findings:* **Syntactic validity and security compliance are "largely orthogonal".** WizardCoder-33B had a 77.8% validate rate but 0% Checkov compliance. The best result, Claude Opus 4 with detailed security prompting, reached 23.1% Checkov and 92.5% Trivy pass. The authors conclude that prompting alone is insufficient.
- *Relation to us:* This is the closest methodological twin in Terraform: multi-LLM (closed and open), multi-scanner (Checkov and Trivy), security-first. It also shows Checkov and Trivy disagreeing strongly, which supports using several scanners. **Difference:** it covers Terraform, uses a small scenario set, has no human baseline from the same projects and does no runtime deployment.

**[12] Hassan et al. 2026, Findings of ACL: ITAB**
- *Problem:* LLM benchmarks for Ansible are synthetic and not execution-based.
- *Method:* 126 real user-authored Ansible tasks with dynamic execution-based evaluation. 14 open-source LLMs were tested.
- *Findings:* No model exceeded 12% pass@10. Of 1,411 execution failures, 44.87% were state-reconciliation reasoning errors and 24.37% were module-knowledge errors.
- *Relation to us:* Execution exposes failures that text metrics hide, which supports our deployment stage. Its failure taxonomy is a model for our runtime-failure coding. **Difference:** it covers Ansible, not K8s, and does not analyse security.

**[13] Kozachok et al. 2026 (arXiv): Context-Instrumental Data Distillation for K8s Manifest Generation**
- *Problem:* Adapting small language models (≤4B) to generate valid K8s manifests.
- *Method:* Synthetic and reverse-instruction data distilled from real YAML and filtered through validators L1–L4 (YAML parse, **kubeconform --strict** against K8s 1.30 schemas, semantic rules and policy). Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B was fine-tuned with LoRA.
- *Findings:* full-pass@1 was 91.5% (183/200) on a held-out set. Strict output-format requirements mattered more than data volume. It is a pilot: no server-side dry-run, single seed.
- *Relation to us:* It uses kubeconform as a gate in the same way we do and shows that small open models can be made schema-valid. **Difference:** it covers generation quality on synthetic tasks only, with no security scanning of outputs versus humans and no deployment. Relevance is moderate because it is a pilot preprint.

**[14] Ding et al. 2026 (arXiv, submitted 2026-09-23): FDE-Bench**
- *Problem:* Can LLM agents produce deployment configurations that actually run?
- *Method:* 136 tasks (Docker, Compose, Kubernetes; greenfield and repair), graded by four gated binary layers: build, **readiness** (rollout completes and pods stay Ready), behaviour and spec conformance. 7 models were tested (Gemini 3.x Flash, GPT-5.6 variants, Claude Sonnet 5, DeepSeek-V4).
- *Findings:* Task resolution ranged from about 53% to 75% (best: Gemini-3.6-Flash at 75.0%, Claude-Sonnet-5 at 72.8%). **Readiness failures were the largest failure category**, and repair tasks were easier than greenfield ones.
- *Relation to us:* It is very close to our runtime-deployability RQ and uses a similar "rollout and Ready" gate, which is evidence that readiness is where LLM configs break. **Difference:** it is agentic (iterative tool use), has no security or best-practice scanning, no human baseline and no fault-injection or recovery test. It is a very recent preprint, so cite it with care.

**[15] Tabibpour & Dakhilalian, "A Comparative Study of AI-Generated and Human-Written Kubernetes Manifests", Journal of Computing and Security 13(1):73–82, 2026. DOI 10.22108/jcs.2026.148046.1195. (Verified from the PDF you downloaded.)**
- *Problem:* Do AI-generated K8s manifests have different configuration smells from human-written ones?
- *Method:* They re-collected 1,985 of Rahman et al.'s 2,039 human manifests and compared them with the **published measurements** of the 98 ChatGPT manifests from Zhang et al. (MSR 2024, collected in July–August 2023 via DevGPT). They **did not generate any new manifests themselves.** The analysis uses KubeLinter only, on 6 smells, reported as density (per 1,000 lines) and proportion (% of files), plus a balanced-size check with 1,000 random 98-file human subsamples.
- *Findings:* Missing CPU/memory specifications are the most robust weakness of the AI manifests. Missing runAsNonRoot and readOnlyRootFilesystem are common in **both** corpora. In the human corpus, 85.16% of smells occur in Deployment and StatefulSet resources.
- *Relation to us:* It is the closest prior work, and **its Limitations section lists our design as future work almost word for word**:
  1. the AI and human corpora are unmatched and very different in size;
  2. the AI side is a single 2023 ChatGPT snapshot ("re-running generation with newer models and documented prompt strategies is left to future work");
  3. only one tool is used ("future work should replicate with kube-score, Checkov, Kubescape, Trivy, Polaris");
  4. **nothing is executed in a cluster** ("a controlled deployment-based validation would be valuable").

  Our study addresses all four. Cite these limitations directly in the Introduction and Research Gap.

**[15b] Zhang, Meredith, Reeves, Coriolano, Babar, Rahman, "Does Generative AI Generate Smells Related to Container Orchestration?: An Exploratory Study with Kubernetes Manifests", MSR 2024 (Lisbon), pp. 192–196. DOI 10.1145/3643991.3645079. (Verified from the full PDF.)**
- *Problem:* How often do ChatGPT-generated K8s manifests contain smells (RQ1), and which object kinds are affected (RQ2)?
- *Method:* Manifests were mined from the 6 DevGPT snapshots (27 Jul – 31 Aug 2023), keeping entries that mention "kubernetes" and YAML snippets with a `kind` field. Prompts were open-coded. **Only KubeLinter** was used, covering 6 smells: Dangling Service, Absent Anti-affinity, Absent Read-only FS, Run as Non-root, Unset CPU req., Unset Memory req. Metrics were density (per KLOC), proportion and temporal proportion. The first author manually checked 50 smells and found 0 false positives.
- *Findings:* 218 smells, 146.0 per KLOC. The most frequent smells were Unset CPU and Unset Memory (68 each, 35.8% of manifests), then Read-only FS and Non-root (34 each). Dangling Service was 10.5% and Anti-affinity 2.7%. Only Deployment and Service objects were affected, and later snapshots had higher smell proportions.
- *Internal inconsistencies worth knowing:*
  1. The abstract says **98** manifests, but the Results and Table 1 say **95**.
  2. The abstract headline is "**35.8%** have ≥1 smell", but Table 2's "All Categories" proportion is **46.3%**.

  When citing, use the 35.8% figure together with its per-smell meaning, or say "35.8–46.3%". Don't mix the two.
- *Threats they list:* DevGPT only, a single tool (KubeLinter), manifests not necessarily used in production, and no other LLMs (they name Llama).
- *Their stated future work:* "(ii) a **comparative evaluation of generative AI techniques** with respect to generating smells". This is exactly our RQ3 (LLM vs LLM).
- *Relation to us:* It is the **seminal study of LLM-generated K8s manifests** and the origin of Tabibpour et al.'s AI baseline. Its limitations are:
  - uncontrolled prompts taken from the wild, of unknown quality;
  - one model, from 2023;
  - one tool, with 6 checks;
  - static-only;
  - no matched human baseline.

  **Our design addresses each of these.** Run KubeLinter's same 6 checks in our pipeline so our 2026 numbers can be put directly next to theirs from 2023.

### Theme (b): security of LLM-generated code

**[16] Pearce et al. 2022, IEEE S&P: Asleep at the Keyboard?**
- *Method:* 89 CWE-oriented scenarios (MITRE Top-25) given to GitHub Copilot, producing 1,689 programs, checked with CodeQL and manual review, across weakness, prompt and domain diversity.
- *Findings:* About 40% of the generated programs were vulnerable.
- *Relation to us:* This is the seminal evidence that LLM assistants reproduce insecure patterns. We extend the question to declarative K8s configurations, where "insecure by omission" (for example, a missing securityContext) is the typical failure. **Difference:** it covers imperative code (C and Python), a single model and no human baseline.

**[17] Fu et al. 2025, TOSEM: Security Weaknesses of Copilot-Generated Code in GitHub Projects**
- *Method:* 733 AI-generated snippets (Copilot, CodeWhisperer, Codeium) found in real GitHub projects and analysed with static analysers.
- *Findings:* 29.5% of Python and 24.2% of JavaScript snippets had weaknesses spanning 43 CWEs, 8 of them in the 2023 CWE Top-25. Copilot Chat fixed up to 55.5% of them.
- *Relation to us:* It studies AI code "in the wild" rather than in controlled prompts, a complementary design to our controlled-spec approach. **Difference:** imperative code only.

**[18] Khoury et al. 2023, IEEE SMC: How Secure is Code Generated by ChatGPT?**
- *Method:* ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) was asked to write 21 programs in 5 languages, each targeting a specific vulnerability class. The authors then prompted it to fix them.
- *Findings:* Only 5 of 21 programs were initially secure, and 7 of the remaining 16 were fixed after prompting. ChatGPT "knows" the vulnerabilities but does not apply that knowledge unprompted.
- *Relation to us:* The "aware but not applied" finding motivates our neutral-spec design, which deliberately does not ask for security. We could optionally add a "security-aware prompt" ablation. **Difference:** small scale and imperative code.

**[19] Bhatt et al. 2023 (Meta, arXiv): CyberSecEval**
- *Method:* The Insecure Code Detector (189 static rules, 50 CWEs) provides automated autocomplete and instruct tests for insecure code, plus cyber-attack helpfulness tests. It covers Llama 2, Code Llama and GPT models. ICD precision is 96% and recall 79%.
- *Findings:* LLMs suggested vulnerable code about 30% of the time on average, and **more capable models tended to suggest insecure code more often.**
- *Relation to us:* It is a standard large-scale benchmark to cite for the insecure-code tendency of open-weight models such as Llama. **Difference:** general-purpose languages, not IaC.

**[20] Vero et al. 2025, ICML: BaxBench**
- *Method:* 392 backend-generation tasks (28 scenarios × 14 frameworks × 6 languages). Functional tests plus **end-to-end executed security exploits**.
- *Findings:* The best model (OpenAI o1) reached only 62% correctness. **Around half of the functionally correct programs were exploitable.** Less popular frameworks were worse.
- *Relation to us:* It is the best example of combining functional correctness with runtime security evaluation, which is analogous to our static-scan plus deploy plus pod-kill design. It also shows that "works" does not mean "secure". **Difference:** application code, not deployment configuration.

### Theme (c): K8s/IaC misconfigurations, smells, defects and tools

**[21] Rahman, Parnin, Williams 2019, ICSE: The Seven Sins. NOT DOWNLOADED (no open-access PDF found; DOI 10.1109/ICSE.2019.00033).**
- *Annotation based on the abstract and verified metadata only:* qualitative analysis of 1,726 Puppet scripts identified 7 security smells: admin by default, empty password, hard-coded secret, invalid IP binding, suspicious comment, HTTP without TLS and weak crypto. The SLIC linter was applied to 15,232 scripts from 293 repos and found 21,201 smell occurrences, including 1,326 hard-coded passwords.
- *Relation to us:* It is the foundational "security smells in IaC" framing. Several smells (hard-coded secrets, HTTP without TLS) reappear in K8s ([1]) and are things we expect in LLM output. **Difference:** Puppet, human-written only.

**[22] Shamim, Bhuiyan, Rahman 2020, IEEE SecDev: XI Commandments of Kubernetes Security**
- *Method:* A grey-literature systematisation of 104 Internet artifacts.
- *Findings:* 11 K8s security practices, including RBAC for least privilege, security patches, pod-specific and network-specific security policies, and namespace separation.
- *Relation to us:* It is the practitioner-grounded justification for which checks matter (securityContext, NetworkPolicy, RBAC). **Difference:** it catalogues practices and does no measurement.

**[23] Zhang, Paul, d'Amorim, Rahman 2026, IEEE TSE 52(3): Configuration Defects in Kubernetes**
- *Method:* Open coding of 719 defects from 2,260 K8s configuration scripts in 185 OSS repos, evaluation of 8 static analysis tools, and a new linter.
- *Findings:* 15 defect categories, 6 of them unique to K8s: custom resources, namespaces, orphanism, pod scheduling, property annotation and volume mounting. The categories include resource, probing and data-field defects. The 8 tools detect only 8 of the 15 categories (best on data fields). Their linter found 26 new defects confirmed by practitioners, 19 of them fixed.
- *Relation to us:* This is the key reference for the **reliability (non-security) side** of our study: probes, resources and scheduling. It also explains why static tools under-detect reliability defects and why we add runtime deployment and pod-kill tests. **Difference:** human-written configurations only, no LLMs.

**[24] Minna, Massacci, Tuma 2025, EMSE 30(5) (registered report at MSR 2024)**
- *Method:* Mined Artifact Hub, downloading 12,965 charts, leaving 8,960 after de-duplication, of which 5,138 rendered with `helm template`. These were analysed with Checkov, KICS, Datree and other tools. LLMs (GPT, Claude, Gemini and others) were asked to refactor each misconfiguration, the result was re-scanned, and a sample was checked manually.
- *Findings:* The most common misconfigurations are environment-dependent (LimitRange/ResourceQuota, NetworkPolicies) or hard (AppArmor/seccomp profiles). The tools disagree. LLMs differ significantly, and GPT satisfied the tools most often, but not consistently. **LLMs can generate correct fixes but may delete unrelated configuration and break the application.** Manual validation showed low refactoring correctness.
- *Relation to us:* It gives strong evidence that scanner-satisfying output is not the same as working output, which directly motivates our deployment stage. It also shows scanner disagreement, which justifies using several tools. **Difference:** repair of Helm charts, not generation, and no cluster deployment.

**[25] Krieger et al. 2026 (arXiv, Dynatrace Research): Comparison of K8s Compliance Standards and Configuration Scanners**
- *Method:* Compared 8 hardening guidelines, including CIS, NSA/CISA, DISA STIG and PCI. Built a benchmark of 79 configuration recommendations and evaluated 10 scanners: Checkov, KICS, kube-score, KubeLinter, Kubescape, Kubesec, Polaris, Snyk, Terrascan and Trivy.
- *Findings:* **Only Trivy and KICS reached F1 ≥ 0.69 with ≥ 50% coverage.** Polaris was competitive with fewer than 50 rules. Kubescape has the most rules but not the best F1 or coverage. No scanner exceeded 40% coverage on RBAC or service-account issues or 50% on NetworkPolicies. Severity scoring was inconsistent across tools.
- *Relation to us:* This is essential for **justifying and interpreting our tool selection** (Kubescape, Trivy, Checkov, kube-score, Polaris). We should report per-tool results and triangulate rather than trust any single scanner, and acknowledge that RBAC and NetworkPolicy issues are under-detected. Preprint.

**[26] Kapetanidou, Nizamis, Votis 2025, MECC '25 (ACM workshop): Evaluation of K8s Security Scanning Tools**
- *Method:* Six tools (Trivy and Kubescape in CLI and operator modes, KubeLinter, Terrascan, Checkov, Kubesec), compared on scan time, number of detected threats and resource usage.
- *Findings:* Workload (in-cluster) scanners surface additional runtime-related risks, with higher but not prohibitive scan time and resource use. The authors recommend combining static analysers with workload scanners across the deployment lifecycle.
- *Relation to us:* Practical support for combining static scanning with in-cluster checks. It is a short workshop paper with low rigour, so cite it only as supporting evidence.

### Theme (d): LLMs for K8s operations and security (beyond the originals)

**[27] Ghorab & Saied 2025, IEEE CLOUD: Unveiling K8s Misconfigurations with LLMs**
- *Method:* A taxonomy built from Stack Overflow posts. Four datasets were labelled by Snyk, kube-score and Datree (the largest has 276,520 object entries). BERT, CodeBERT, RoBERTa and Llama-3 were fine-tuned for detection and compared with rule-based tools.
- *Findings:* The fine-tuned Llama-3 detects more issues than the rule-based tools (for example, 1,436 in Deployments). It is strong on probe and resource issues and weaker on access-control and network semantics.
- *Relation to us:* It supports taxonomy categories for probes and resources, which are reliability-relevant. **Difference:** detection only, no generation.

**[28] Ghorab, Abdellatif, Saied 2026, AIware (per arXiv comment): K8s Misconfigurations in the Wild / Kubecurity**
- *Method:* A taxonomy from 2,613 relevant Stack Overflow posts (2,662 in the abstract). Its categories include Access & Privileges (23.8%), Resource Management & Probes (21.5%) and Image & Network Security (19.0%). An evolution analysis of 73 Helm charts from the incubator to the stable stage. LLM repair used Mistral-7B, GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-5.4, Llama-3.1-70B, DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen2.5-7B with progressively richer context, plus Kubecurity for schema-guided correction.
- *Findings:* Only 21.43% of 336 incubator misconfigurations were fixed in stable releases, and 6.25% were newly introduced. Richer context raised LLM repair accuracy (best standalone 89.06%). The hybrid with schema enforcement reached 98.5%.
- *Relation to us:* Evidence that human-maintained charts keep misconfigurations even as they mature, which matters for interpreting our human baseline. Schema validation (like kubeconform) substantially fixes LLM structural errors. **Difference:** repair, not generation, with no runtime evaluation.

**[29] Shaikh 2026, LLMSec @ ESORICS (accepted): KuTIE, runtime topology context for LLM K8s patches**
- *Method:* LLM remediation of Trivy KSPM findings, conditioned on live Istio call edges and service-account bindings, with each patch gated on its functional blast radius. Tested on a 36-deployment healthcare cluster with 31 findings across 7 dependency classes.
- *Findings:* For topology-dependent cases, patch correctness rose from 11.1% without context to 78.0% with it. Context had negligible effect on topology-independent controls.
- *Relation to us:* Direct evidence that **LLM K8s configs that pass scanners can still break running services**, which is exactly what our deploy and pod-kill stage measures. **Difference:** patching, not generation, from a single author, as a workshop paper.

## 4. Research gap

The following gap is grounded in the papers read above.

1. **Generation benchmarks are task-level and do not compare against humans.** CloudEval-YAML [5], IaC-Eval [6], DPIaC-Eval [9], TerraFormer [10], ITAB [12], Kozachok [13] and FDE-Bench [14] score LLM output against a reference or unit test on synthetic or curated *tasks*. None of them takes **real open-source projects**, writes a neutral spec, and compares LLM output with **the human-written manifests of the same project**. So none can answer "are LLM manifests better or worse than what developers actually ship?"
2. **The only human-vs-AI K8s comparison found is static and unmatched.** The J. Computing & Security 2026 study [15] compares 1,985 human manifests with 98 AI manifests from different sources. By its own account it runs no manifests in a cluster. Pairing each LLM output with the human manifest for the same spec, across several LLMs, is new.
3. **Security studies of LLM configurations are Terraform/AWS-centric and static.** Santos Vargas et al. [11] and IaCGen [9] show that validity and deployability are largely unrelated to security compliance (0–23% Checkov compliance, 8.4% security compliance). This has not been measured for Kubernetes workloads across GPT, Claude, Gemini and open-weight models with a multi-scanner (Kubescape, Trivy, Checkov) pipeline.
4. **Reliability is almost absent.** K8s LLM work focuses on security misconfigurations ([2], [3], [4], [24], [27]–[29]). Reliability best practices are studied only for human configurations ([23] and, partly, [1]): probes, resource requests and limits, replicas, PodDisruptionBudgets and anti-affinity. No study measures them in LLM-generated manifests with kube-score or Polaris.
5. **No study tests runtime resilience.** CloudEval-YAML [5] and FDE-Bench [14] check that YAML deploys or reaches Ready. KubeGuard [4] and KuTIE [29] use live clusters for hardening or patching. None **injects failures such as pod kills and measures recovery** (time to Ready, service availability) as a property of the generated configuration. Replicas, probes and PDBs determine this behaviour, and static tools cannot observe it.
6. **Static and runtime views are not linked.** Minna et al. [24], Shaikh [29] and Krieger et al. [25] show that scanner verdicts are inconsistent and that scanner-passing configurations can break applications. No prior work puts the **same configurations** through schema, security, best-practice, deploy and recovery layers to see how static findings relate to runtime outcomes.
7. **LLM-vs-LLM evidence for K8s is thin and outdated.** CloudEval-YAML used GPT-4 and Llama-2-era models. LLMSecConfig [3] compared two models, for repair. A systematic comparison of current frontier and open-weight models (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral) on K8s *generation* quality is missing.

**Positioning statement:** our study is, to our knowledge, the first **end-to-end, project-matched comparison of human-written and multi-LLM-generated Kubernetes manifests**. The same neutral specifications and the same five-layer pipeline cover schema (kubeconform), security (Kubescape, Trivy, Checkov), reliability best practices (kube-score, Polaris), actual deployment (kind or minikube) and pod-kill recovery. The design ties the misconfiguration taxonomies of Rahman et al. [1] and Zhang et al. [23] to measured runtime behaviour.

- Several of the most relevant works are 2026 preprints ([13], [14], [25]) or weeks-old accepted papers ([11], [28], [29]). Re-check their venue status before submission.
- [15] and [15b] were verified from the full PDFs.
- GenKubeSec [2] and KubeGuard [4] are cited as arXiv preprints. No peer-reviewed venue could be confirmed.
