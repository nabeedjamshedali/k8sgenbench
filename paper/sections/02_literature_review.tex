\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:literature}

This section reviews prior work in six areas that together frame this study. Section~\ref{sec:lit-human} covers misconfigurations and defects in human-written Kubernetes configurations. Section~\ref{sec:lit-tools} covers the standards and scanners used to assess them. Section~\ref{sec:lit-llmcode} covers the security of LLM-generated code. Section~\ref{sec:lit-iac} covers LLM-based generation of infrastructure and cloud configurations. Section~\ref{sec:lit-llmk8s} covers existing studies of LLM-generated Kubernetes manifests, and Section~\ref{sec:lit-llmsec} covers the use of LLMs to detect, repair and harden Kubernetes configurations. Section~\ref{sec:lit-gap} summarises the research gap.

\subsection{Misconfigurations in human-written Kubernetes configurations}\label{sec:lit-human}

Security problems in infrastructure-as-code (IaC) were first characterised systematically by Rahman et al., who identified seven security smells in Puppet scripts, including hard-coded secrets, admin-by-default settings and the use of HTTP without TLS, and detected more than 21,000 smell occurrences in over 15,000 scripts \citep{rahman2019sevensins}. For Kubernetes specifically, Shamim et al.\ systematised practitioner knowledge from 104 Internet artefacts into eleven recommended security practices, such as enforcing role-based access control for least privilege, applying pod- and network-specific security policies and separating workloads into namespaces \citep{shamim2020xicommandments}.

The most comprehensive empirical study of Kubernetes security misconfigurations analysed 2,039 manifests from 92 open-source repositories \citep{rahman2023k8smisconfig}. It identified 11 misconfiguration categories: absent resource limits, absent security context, activation of hostIPC, hostNetwork and hostPID, capability misuse, Docker-socket mounting, privilege escalation, hard-coded secrets, insecure HTTP and privileged security contexts. In total, 1,051 misconfigurations affected 13.9\% of the analysed Kubernetes objects, and larger manifests were more likely to be misconfigured. Beyond security, Zhang et al.\ studied 719 configuration defects in 2,260 Kubernetes configuration scripts from 185 repositories \citep{zhang2026k8sconfigdefects}. They derived 15 defect categories, six of which are unique to Kubernetes (e.g., pod scheduling, volume mounting and orphaned resources), and showed that eight static analysis tools could detect only 8 of the 15 categories. Studies of Helm charts point the same way. Minna et al.\ analysed over 5,000 rendered Helm charts from Artifact Hub and found that the most frequent misconfigurations concern resource quotas, network policies and hard-to-configure security profiles \citep{minna2025helm}. Ghorab et al.\ observed that only 21.43\% of the misconfigurations present in incubator Helm charts were fixed by the time the charts reached the stable stage \citep{ghorab2026inthewild}.

These studies establish that human-written configurations are far from flawless and offer ready taxonomies for classifying problems. However, they examine only human-authored artefacts, and nearly all rely on static analysis without observing how the configurations behave when deployed.

\subsection{Standards and tools for assessing Kubernetes configurations}\label{sec:lit-tools}

Several authoritative guidelines define what a secure Kubernetes configuration should look like, including the NSA/CISA Kubernetes Hardening Guide \citep{nsacisa2022k8shardening}, the CIS Kubernetes Benchmark \citep{cis_k8s_benchmark}, the OWASP Kubernetes Top Ten \citep{owasp_k8s_top10} and the Kubernetes Pod Security Standards, which define \emph{privileged}, \emph{baseline} and \emph{restricted} profiles for pods \citep{k8s_pod_security_standards}. These guidelines are operationalised by open-source static scanners. Examples include Kubescape \citep{tool_kubescape}, Trivy \citep{tool_trivy} and Checkov \citep{tool_checkov}, which focus mainly on security, and kube-score \citep{tool_kubescore}, Polaris \citep{tool_polaris} and KubeLinter \citep{tool_kubelinter}, which also cover reliability best practices such as probes, resource requests and replica counts. Schema validators such as kubeconform check manifests against the official Kubernetes API schemas \citep{tool_kubeconform}.

Evidence on the consistency of these tools is, however, mixed. Krieger et al.\ compared eight compliance standards and ten configuration scanners on a benchmark of 79 recommendations \citep{krieger2026k8sscanners}. Only two scanners reached an F1-score of at least 0.69 while covering at least half of the recommendations, and severity ratings were inconsistent across tools. Kapetanidou et al.\ similarly recommended combining static analysers with in-cluster workload scanning \citep{kapetanidou2025k8sscanning}, and Lanciano et al.\ used Polaris findings as quality labels for manifests \citep{lanciano2023declarative}. Two implications follow for empirical studies: relying on a single scanner risks tool-specific bias, and static verdicts need to be complemented by observations of runtime behaviour.

\subsection{Security of LLM-generated code}\label{sec:lit-llmcode}

A substantial body of work shows that LLM code assistants frequently produce insecure code. Pearce et al.\ prompted GitHub Copilot with 89 scenarios derived from the MITRE CWE Top-25 and found that approximately 40\% of the 1,689 generated programs were vulnerable \citep{pearce2022asleep}. Khoury et al.\ reported that only 5 of 21 programs written by ChatGPT were initially secure, even though the model could explain the relevant vulnerabilities when asked, meaning that it had security knowledge but did not apply it unprompted \citep{khoury2023chatgptsecure}. In real projects, Fu et al.\ found security weaknesses in 29.5\% of Python and 24.2\% of JavaScript snippets generated by AI assistants and committed to GitHub, spanning 43 CWE categories \citep{fu2025copilotweaknesses}. At scale, the CyberSecEval benchmark showed that LLMs suggested insecure code in roughly 30\% of test cases, with more capable models tending to produce insecure code more often \citep{bhatt2023cyberseceval}. BaxBench combined functional tests with executed security exploits and found that even the best model generated correct backends in only 62\% of cases, and that around half of the correct programs were exploitable \citep{vero2025baxbench}.

These studies consistently show that functional correctness does not imply security, and that LLMs tend to omit security measures unless explicitly asked. They focus, however, on imperative application code. In declarative configurations, security and reliability depend largely on \emph{what is omitted}, such as a missing security context, probe or resource limit, and this kind of failure has received far less attention.

\subsection{LLM-based generation of infrastructure and cloud configurations}\label{sec:lit-iac}

Early work on LLM generation of declarative IT configurations includes Ansible Wisdom, which fine-tuned a code model on Ansible YAML and evaluated it with text-similarity and schema-correctness metrics \citep{pujar2023ansiblewisdom}. CloudEval-YAML introduced 1,011 hand-written cloud-native configuration problems (Kubernetes, Envoy and Istio), each paired with a unit test executed on a real cluster \citep{xu2024cloudevalyaml}. Across 12 models, the best model (GPT-4) achieved a unit-test score of only 0.515, and open models of the time performed considerably worse. For Terraform, IaC-Eval provided 458 AWS scenarios with intent specifications, on which the best model achieved a pass@1 of only 19.36\% \citep{kon2024iaceval}, while TerraFormer improved generation through fine-tuning with feedback from formal verifiers and policy checks \citep{jana2026terraformer}.

More recent studies emphasise deployability and security. Zhang et al.\ evaluated LLM-generated AWS CloudFormation templates by actually deploying them and found first-attempt deployment success of only 20.8--30.2\% across six LLMs. Even after iterative refinement, requirement coverage (25.2\%) and security compliance (8.4\%) remained low \citep{zhang2026iacgen}. Santos Vargas et al.\ benchmarked seven proprietary and open-weight models on Terraform generation with Checkov and Trivy and concluded that syntactic validity and security compliance are ``largely orthogonal'': the best configuration reached only 23.1\% Checkov compliance \citep{santosvargas2026secureterraform}. For Ansible, the execution-based ITAB benchmark showed that no open-source model exceeded 12\% pass@10 on real user tasks \citep{hassan2026itab}. For Kubernetes, Kozachok et al.\ used staged validators, including kubeconform, to adapt small language models to manifest generation \citep{kozachok2026k8sdistillation}. FDE-Bench evaluated LLM agents on 136 deployment-configuration tasks, including Kubernetes, using gated build, readiness, behaviour and conformance checks, with resolution rates of 52.9--75.0\% and readiness identified as the dominant failure stage \citep{ding2026fdebench}.

Together, these works establish execution-based validation as the most reliable way to judge LLM-generated infrastructure configurations, and they show that validity, deployability and security must be measured separately. Their evaluations, however, are built on curated or synthetic tasks with reference solutions. None of them compares LLM outputs with the configurations that developers actually maintain for real applications, and none examines how the generated configurations behave under failures.

\subsection{Studies of LLM-generated Kubernetes manifests}\label{sec:lit-llmk8s}

Two studies directly examine the quality of LLM-generated Kubernetes manifests. Zhang et al.\ mined 98 ChatGPT-generated manifests from developer conversations in the DevGPT dataset collected in July and August 2023, and analysed them with KubeLinter \citep{zhang2024genaismells}. Unset CPU and memory requirements were the most frequent smells, each affecting 35.8\% of the manifests, followed by missing read-only root file systems and containers not required to run as non-root. The authors explicitly called for ``a comparative evaluation of generative AI techniques'', and noted that their findings were limited to one model, one tool and manifests of unknown provenance. Tabibpour and Dakhilalian then compared 1,985 human-written manifests from the corpus of Rahman et al.\ with the published measurements of those 98 ChatGPT manifests, using six KubeLinter smells and a size-balanced resampling analysis \citep{jcomsec2026aivshumank8s}. Missing CPU and memory specifications emerged as the most robust weakness of the AI-generated manifests, whereas missing \texttt{runAsNonRoot} and \texttt{readOnlyRootFilesystem} settings were common in both corpora.

These studies provide the first evidence that LLM-generated manifests inherit and sometimes amplify known weaknesses. The authors themselves acknowledge several limitations. The human and AI corpora are unmatched and differ greatly in size, and the AI corpus reflects a single 2023 model rather than current LLMs. The analysis relies on a single tool, and no manifest is executed in a cluster \citep{jcomsec2026aivshumank8s}.

\subsection{LLMs for detecting, repairing and hardening Kubernetes configurations}\label{sec:lit-llmsec}

A separate line of research uses LLMs to analyse or improve existing Kubernetes configurations. GenKubeSec fine-tuned models to detect, localise, explain and remediate misconfigurations across 276,520 configuration files. It matched rule-based tools in precision and exceeded them in recall, whereas off-the-shelf models, including ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, detected misconfigurations poorly (best F1 $\approx$ 0.51) \citep{malul2024genkubesec}. Ghorab and Saied showed that a fine-tuned Llama-3 model detected more issues than rule-based tools, particularly for probes and resources \citep{ghorab2025k8sllm}. For repair, LLMSecConfig combined Checkov findings with retrieval-augmented generation and reported large differences between models, with a 94.3\% repair success rate for Mistral Large 2 against 40.2\% for GPT-4o-mini \citep{ye2025llmsecconfig}. Minna et al.\ found that LLM-produced fixes for Helm chart misconfigurations can satisfy scanners while deleting unrelated configuration and breaking the application \citep{minna2025helm}. Ghorab et al.\ showed that schema-guided correction raises LLM repair accuracy to 98.5\% \citep{ghorab2026inthewild}. Runtime context has also been explored: KubeGuard derived least-privilege roles, network policies and deployments from runtime logs \citep{sgancohen2025kubeguard}, and KuTIE showed that LLM security patches that ignore the live service topology are correct in only 11.1\% of topology-dependent cases \citep{shaikh2026kutie}.

These works demonstrate both the potential of LLMs for Kubernetes security and their inconsistency across models. Crucially, they also show that scanner-compliant configurations can still break running services. All of them, however, start from an existing manifest. None asks how good an LLM is when it must author the complete configuration of an application from a requirements description, which is how practitioners increasingly use these models.

\subsection{Research gap}\label{sec:lit-gap}

Table~\ref{tab:related} compares the most closely related studies with the present work. The review reveals five gaps.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Real-project comparison.} Existing generation benchmarks evaluate curated tasks, and the only human-versus-AI Kubernetes comparison uses unmatched corpora. No study compares LLM-generated configurations with the human-written configurations of the \emph{same} applications, generated from the same requirements.
    \item \textbf{Current LLMs.} Evidence on LLM-generated Kubernetes manifests relies on a single 2023 ChatGPT corpus. Current proprietary and open-weight models have not been systematically compared, and neither has their consistency across repeated generations.
    \item \textbf{Reliability.} Research on LLM-generated configurations concentrates on security. Reliability controls, such as probes, resource requests, replicas, disruption budgets and anti-affinity, have been studied only for human-written configurations.
    \item \textbf{Runtime resilience.} Prior work stops at static analysis or, at most, at checking whether a configuration reaches a ready state. No study injects failures to measure whether generated configurations recover and keep the service available.
    \item \textbf{Linking static and runtime evidence.} Scanners disagree with each other, and scanner-compliant configurations can still fail at runtime. Yet no study evaluates the same configurations across static correctness, security, reliability, deployment and recovery to test whether static findings predict runtime behaviour.
\end{enumerate}
This study addresses these gaps through a project-matched comparison of human-written configurations and configurations generated by five current LLMs, evaluated with a six-dimension benchmark that spans static analysis, requirement satisfaction, real deployment and fault injection.
